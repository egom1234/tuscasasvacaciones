What to see in Menorca

This guide goes beyond your accommodation and covers the whole island: from Cap d'Artrutx and Binibeca Vell to Ciutadella, Mahón and the unspoilt coves of the south. Everything you need to make the most of your stay at either Casa Gonda or Loft Binibeca.

Your base: Cap d'Artrutx and Binibeca Vell

Cap d'Artrutx, where Casa Gonda is located, is part of the municipality of Ciutadella, on Menorca's western tip, next to developments like Cala en Bosc and Son Xoriguer. Binibeca Vell, where Loft Binibeca is located, belongs to the municipality of Sant Lluís, in the southeast of the island: a development built to resemble a traditional fishing village, with whitewashed houses, arches and narrow lanes that have made it one of Menorca's most photographed corners.

Cap d'Artrutx Lighthouse

Marks Menorca's south-western tip. An easy walk along the sea, especially beautiful at sunset.

Getting lost in Binibeca Vell

Whitewashed houses, arches, flower pots and a small navigable canal. You can walk the whole village in under half an hour, unhurried.

Nearby coves

Cala en Bosc and Son Xoriguer from Cap d'Artrutx; Binibeca's own beach, Cala Torret, Cala Binissafúller and Punta Prima from Binibeca. Calm waters, great for families.

Ciutadella, the former capital

On the western tip of the island, Ciutadella was Menorca's capital until the English occupation of 1714 and still holds the island's most monumental old town: narrow streets like Ses Voltes, the Plaça des Born with its obelisk, and the Gothic cathedral of Santa María, built between the 13th and 14th centuries. Its harbour, tucked between white houses, is one of the prettiest on the island.

Old town and cathedral

Cobbled streets, stately palaces and the cathedral of Santa María presiding over the city's highest point.

Ciutadella's harbour

A narrow, elongated natural harbour lined with restaurant terraces at the water's edge — a great spot for dinner watching the boats go by.

Naveta des Tudons

On the Ciutadella–Maó road, one of Menorca's best-known prehistoric funerary monuments, unique to the island.

Mahón, the current capital

On the eastern side of the island, Mahón (Maó) has been Menorca's administrative capital since the 18th century and has one of the longest natural harbours in the Mediterranean, around six kilometres long. The 18th-century British rule left its mark on the architecture, with Georgian-style façades and sash windows rarely seen elsewhere in Spain.

Port of Mahón

One of the largest natural harbours in the world, with historic islets inside it such as Isla del Rey.

Georgian architecture

A legacy of a century of British rule: façades, balconies and sash windows not seen elsewhere in Spain.

The birthplace of mayonnaise

Mahonnaise sauce may well have originated here, according to popular tradition tied to the French capture of the city in 1756.

Unspoilt coves of the south

The stretch of coastline south of Ciutadella, past Cala en Bosc, holds some of Menorca's least developed coves: turquoise water between pine woods, with barely any buildings around.

Cala Turqueta

One of Menorca's most iconic postcard spots: white sand and clear water framed by pine trees.

Cala Macarelleta and Macarella

Two twin coves linked by a clifftop path; Macarelleta, the smaller one, tends to be a bit quieter.

Festivals and tradition

Sant Joan de Ciutadella

In late June, Menorca's best-known festival: horses rearing on their hind legs through the crowds in Ciutadella's old town, in the tradition known as "es jaleo".

Fiestas de Gracia (Mahón)

From 6 to 9 September, in honour of the Virgen de Gracia: between 150 and 180 Menorcan horses enter the town hall square four at a time, in the traditional jaleo.

Sant Lluís festivals

The last weekend of August, with the traditional jaleos on Saturday afternoon and Sunday morning, plus concerts, parade floats and fireworks.
